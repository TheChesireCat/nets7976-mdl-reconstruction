Week 4 · Network reconstruction from noisy data II

Who swims with whom, and how sure are we?

Thirteen male bottlenose dolphins, and a count of how often each pair was seen swimming together. Young, Cantwell and Newman turn those counts into a posterior over networks. Every quantity on this page is recomputed in your browser from the real data, with a hand-written HMC sampler standing in for Stan. If you haven't yet, the primer builds each step on a toy version of this model.

The map

§2 and Appendix A on one page. Click a box to jump to its section.

Observed: counts \(X_{ij}\). Latent: the network \(A\). Parameters: \(\theta = (\lambda_0, \lambda_1, \rho)\). The trick of the paper is the order of the bottom row: sample \(\theta\) with \(A\) summed out, then draw networks given each \(\theta\).

Three modelling choices

Eqs. (2.1)–(2.3), (3.1)–(3.3)
\(P(\theta)\)network model \(P(A \mid \theta) = \prod \nu_{ij}(A_{ij})\)data model \(P(X \mid A, \theta) = \prod \mu_{ij}(A_{ij})\)the user's only input

The paper asks for three choices and does the rest automatically. The data model says what a count looks like for an edge and for a non-edge. The network model is the prior on edges. Then there is a prior on \(\theta\). Pairs are conditionally independent given \(\theta\), which is what makes everything below exact apart from the sampling.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{data model} \quad && \mu_{ij}(1) = \Poisson(X_{ij} \mid \lambda_1) \quad && \mu_{ij}(0) = \Poisson(X_{ij} \mid \lambda_0) \quad && \lambda_1 > \lambda_0 \quad \text{(breaks the symmetry)} \\ &\text{network model} \quad && \nu_{ij}(1) = \rho && \nu_{ij}(0) = 1 - \rho \\ &\text{prior on } \theta \quad && \rho \sim \Uniform(0, 1) && \lambda_k \sim \Normal^{+}(1, 100) && \text{(the authors' code; the text says half-normal, } \sigma = 100\text{)} \end{aligned} \]
Why \(\lambda_1 > \lambda_0\)? Without it, "edge" and "non-edge" can swap names (\(\lambda_0 \leftrightarrow \lambda_1\), \(\rho \leftrightarrow 1 - \rho\)) and nothing in the likelihood changes. The primer's §6 shows what that does to the chains.
3

Sampling \(\theta\) with the network summed out

Eqs. (A.11)–(A.12) · §3.1.2 · Figs. 3a, 3b
\(P(A, \theta \mid X)\)sum over \(A\) (one sum per pair) \(P(\theta \mid X) \propto P(\theta) \prod_{ij} \sum_k \mu_{ij}(k)\, \nu_{ij}(k)\)MCMC \(\theta^{(1)}, \dots, \theta^{(m)}\)exact sum · sampled

Four chains start at random points. Pairs with the same count contribute identical factors, so the product only runs over the 17 distinct counts (§A.6.2). Switch the sampler in the top bar. At the same number of iterations Metropolis leaves \(\hat{R}\) well above 1.01 and ends up with tens of effective draws, where HMC has hundreds. That holds even per unit of work, although each Metropolis step is about ten times cheaper.

Log density along the chains

The density the sampler moves on: \(\log P(\theta \mid X)\) on the unconstrained scale, plus the log-Jacobian of the transforms, like Stan's lp__. After warmup (shaded) the four chains should look like the same noise (Fig. 3a).

Pair plot

Marginal histograms on the diagonal and joint draws off it, coloured by chain (Fig. 3b).

Compare the paper: \(\lambda_0 = 0.62\) [0.06, 0.98], \(\lambda_1 = 14.3\) [10.8, 16.8], \(\rho = 0.28\) [0.18, 0.43]. A connected pair interacts about 14 times, an unconnected one less than once. \(\lambda_0\) and \(\rho\) are negatively correlated: if more pairs are allowed to be edges, fewer low counts have to be explained by the non-edge rate.
4

Networks from \(\theta\)

Eqs. (A.13)–(A.14), (2.4) · Fig. 2
\(\theta^{(r)}\)condition \(Q_{ij}(k, \theta^{(r)}) = \mu_{ij}(k)\,\nu_{ij}(k) \big/ \sum_{k'} \mu_{ij}(k')\,\nu_{ij}(k')\)draw each pair \(A^{(r)} \sim \prod Q_{ij}\)exact given \(\theta\)

Given \(\theta\), every pair is an independent coin with probability \(Q_{ij}\), so a whole network is drawn by flipping 78 coins. Doing that once per \(\theta\) draw gives samples from the joint posterior \(P(A, \theta \mid X)\). Averaging \(A_{ij}\) over them gives each pair's posterior edge probability.

Two networks from the posterior

Similar but not identical (Fig. 2a). Edges that change between draws are the uncertain ones.

Posterior edge probabilities

\(P(A_{ij} \ge 1 \mid X)\), averaged over all draws (Fig. 2b). Log colour scale. Hover for the count and the spread of \(Q_{ij}\) over \(\theta\).

Counts spread out, probabilities cluster. Counts range from 0 to 31, but almost every edge probability is near 0 or near 1: the model is confident about nearly every pair. With a posterior over whole networks you can also answer network questions, like the number of edges or the triangle count above, with an honest interval.

Is it a good fit?

§3.1.2 · Eq. (3.4) · Fig. 4 · the authors' tutorial, cells 66 and 70
\((A^{(r)}, \theta^{(r)})\)simulate \(\tilde{X} \sim P(\tilde{X} \mid A^{(r)}, \theta^{(r)})\)compare \(p = P\big(D(\tilde{X}; \theta) > D(X; \theta)\big)\)posterior-predictive check

A converged sampler says nothing about whether the model is right. For each of 500 posterior draws, generate a fake count matrix. Then compare the discrepancy \(D = \sum X_{ij} \log\big(X_{ij} / \langle \tilde{X}_{ij} \rangle\big)\) of the fake data with that of the real data, both measured against the model's expected counts \(\langle \tilde{X}_{ij} \rangle = \sum_k Q_{ij}(k)\, \lambda_k\).

Observed vs. predicted interactions

Each dot is a pair, in 5 simulated data sets (Fig. 4a / 5a). Shading marks what the fitted rates call no tie, a weak tie and a strong tie.

Discrepancy of fake vs. real data

One dot per posterior draw. The share above the diagonal is the p-value (Fig. 4b).

With two edge types the fit is so-so (the paper finds \(p = 0.136\)). The predicted counts sit in two bands, near 0 and near 14, while the real ones sit in three groups: around 0, around 5 and around 25. Switch to 3 edge types in the top bar.

Weak and strong ties

§3.1.3 · Eqs. (3.5)–(3.6) · Fig. 5
\(A_{ij} \in \{0, 1, 2\}\)same recipe \(\lambda_0 < \lambda_1 < \lambda_2\), \(\rho\) on the simplexone more rate, one more weight

Nothing in the method assumes edges are binary. Add a third type with its own rate, keep the rates ordered (the symmetry now has \(3! = 6\) relabellings to break), and let \((\rho_0, \rho_1, \rho_2)\) be any probability vector. Everything else is unchanged. This section always shows both models, whatever the top bar says.

The inferred network with three tie types

Thick: strong ties. Thin: weak ties. Line opacity: posterior probability (Fig. 5b).

Discrepancy, two vs. three edge types

The same check as §5, for both models. The more of a cloud sits above the diagonal, the better the fit.

The data have three groups: two tight subgroups (9–10, 11–13) and a larger group mixing weak and strong ties. The paper gets \(p = 0.722\). The one pair the model can't classify is 1–2, seen 12 times, right between the weak (≈5) and strong (≈22) rates. Hover it in the matrix above after switching models.

Full Bayes vs. EM

§3.2 ("semi-Bayesian"), §A.3 · compare Newman 2018
EM: \(Q_{ij}(\hat{\theta})\)vs. full Bayes: \(\int Q_{ij}(\theta)\, P(\theta \mid X)\, d\theta\)same \(Q\), different \(\theta\)

Last week's EM computes the same \(Q_{ij}\) at one point estimate \(\hat{\theta}\). Full Bayes averages it over the posterior of \(\theta\). On 78 pairs with few parameters the per-pair answers agree closely. The difference shows where \(\theta\) is uncertain: pairs whose count sits between the two rates, and totals summed over many pairs.

Per pair: EM's Q vs. the posterior average

Each dot is a count value (all pairs with that count share it). The whiskers are the 90% range of \(Q\) over \(\theta\) draws. EM gives only the dot's x.

In total: how many edges?

Edges per sampled network, with \(\theta\) fixed at \(\hat{\theta}\) (EM) and with \(\theta\) averaged over its posterior (full Bayes).

Full Bayes costs little extra here and buys three things: uncertainty about \(\theta\) carried into every answer, a model check that uses it, and no hand-derived M-step. The last is why swapping in three edge types took one line. For AddHealth the model has two rates per student, over a thousand parameters, and the p-value near 1 hints at overfitting. That is where the paper's suggested beta hyperpriors on the rates would come in.
