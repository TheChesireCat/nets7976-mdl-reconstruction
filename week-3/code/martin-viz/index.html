Week 3 · Network reconstruction from noisy data

Which distribution are you holding?

Every step in Martin, Ball & Newman does one of five things to a probability distribution: it samples from it, constrains it, sums a variable out, maximizes over parameters, or conditions on data. This page follows one synthetic network through the paper and shows, at each step, what goes in, what comes out, and whether the step is exact or approximate. The sliders above change the network, and every chart below recomputes from it.

0

The map

The whole paper on one page. Click a box to jump to its section.

The top row is the story the paper tells about how the data came to be. The bottom row is inference: running that story backwards. Four kinds of symbols appear, and keeping them apart is most of the work: observed (the matrix \(Q\)), latent (the network \(A\) and the groups \(g\)), parameters (\(\gamma\), \(\omega\)), and nuisance (the noise densities \(\beta_0\), \(\beta_1\)).

1

The generative model

Sec. II A · Eq. (1)
parameters \(\gamma, \omega\)sample \(P(A, g \mid \gamma, \omega)\)forward model

The stochastic block model. Each node picks a group with probability \(\gamma_r\) (here \(\gamma = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\)), and each pair is joined with probability \(\omega_{g_i g_j}\): \(\omega_\text{in}\) inside a group, \(\omega_\text{out}\) between groups.

\[ P(A, g \mid \gamma, \omega) = \prod_i \gamma_{g_i} \prod_{i<j} \omega_{g_i g_j}^{A_{ij}} \, (1 - \omega_{g_i g_j})^{1 - A_{ij}} \]

The true network \(A\)

Adjacency matrix, rows and columns sorted by true group. Strips show group A and group B.

What the block structure predicts

Edge density inside and between groups: set by \(\omega\), and measured in this sample.

What you'd estimate here: nothing yet. This is the prior over the two latent objects, the network and the groups. Writing it down is always step one: it is the joint distribution of everything that you will later condition on or sum out.
2

The noise process

Sec. II A · Eqs. (2), (38), (39) · Fig. 2
\(P(A, g \mid \gamma, \omega)\)multiply by \(P(Q \mid A, g, \gamma, \omega) = P(Q \mid A)\) \(P(Q, A, g \mid \gamma, \omega)\)forward model + one assumption

The experiment never shows \(A\). It reports a probability \(Q_{ij}\) for every pair. A true edge draws its \(Q\) from the density \(\beta_1\); a non-edge draws it from \(\beta_0\), which puts most of its mass exactly at zero. The paper's synthetic tests use Beta shapes, and so does this page. Raise \(b_1\) in the top bar and the two densities slide together: the data carry less information about \(A\).

\[ \begin{aligned} P(Q, A, g \mid \gamma, \omega) &= P(Q \mid A, g, \gamma, \omega) \, P(A, g \mid \gamma, \omega) && \leftarrow \text{chain rule} \\ &= P(Q \mid A) \, P(A, g \mid \gamma, \omega) && \leftarrow \text{if } Q \perp (g, \gamma, \omega) \mid A \\[6pt] P(Q \mid A) &= \prod_{i<j} \beta_1(Q_{ij})^{A_{ij}} \, \beta_0(Q_{ij})^{1 - A_{ij}} \\ \beta_1 &= \Beta(a_1, b_1) \qquad \beta_0 = c \cdot \Beta(a_1 - 1, b_1 + 1) + (1 - c) \cdot \delta(Q) \end{aligned} \]

Noise densities

Density of the reported \(Q\) for true edges and for non-edges (the continuous part; the rest of \(\beta_0\) is the spike at 0).

What you actually observe: \(Q\)

Same pair order as \(A\) above. Compare with the true network.

Writing \(P(Q \mid A)\) is a modeling claim, not notation. It says that once you know whether an edge exists, the groups don't change how likely the lab is to report it. If within-group interactions were easier to detect, you would need \(\beta_1(Q \mid g_i, g_j)\) instead. The joint is now complete, and everything later in the paper comes from this one product by conditioning on \(Q\) and summing out what you don't want.
3

Calibration: getting rid of the unknown noise

Sec. II A · Eqs. (3)–(5), (40)
two unknown densities \(\beta_0, \beta_1\)constrain \(\beta_1 / \beta_0 = (Q/\rho) \big/ \big((1 - Q)/(1 - \rho)\big)\)exact, if Q is calibrated

The paper never learns \(\beta_0\) or \(\beta_1\). It assumes the \(Q_{ij}\) are honest probabilities: of all pairs reported at \(Q = 0.3\), 30% really are edges. That single assumption fixes the ratio of the two densities (Eq. 4), and then \(\beta_0\) factors out of the likelihood as a constant (Eq. 6). A nuisance function has been removed without ever being estimated.

The trick is Bayes' rule in odds form. A calibrated \(Q\) is a posterior, and dividing out the prior odds leaves the likelihood ratio: how many times more probable this report is if the edge exists than if it doesn't.

\[ \underbrace{\frac{Q}{1 - Q}}_{\text{posterior odds}} = \underbrace{\frac{\beta_1(Q)}{\beta_0(Q)}}_{\text{likelihood ratio}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\rho}{1 - \rho}}_{\text{prior odds}} \]

Eq. (3): is \(Q\) calibrated?

Fraction of pairs in each \(Q\) bin that are true edges. Calibration means the dots sit on the diagonal.

Eq. (4): what calibration hands the model

The likelihood ratio \(\beta_1/\beta_0\) implied by each \(Q\) (line), and measured from this sample's true edges (dots). Log scale.

How this page generates calibrated noise: fixing \(a_1\) and \(b_1\) and requiring Eq. (4) forces the non-edge sparsity to \(c = \rho\, b_1 \big/ \big((1 - \rho)(a_1 - 1)\big)\). Eq. (40) as printed is the reciprocal of this; simulating with the printed version gives visibly miscalibrated \(Q\), and with the paper's Fig. 3a parameters it would need \(c \approx 11\). If a slider setting would need \(c > 1\), the top bar warns you and calibration no longer holds exactly.

Pattern to reuse: when a piece of the model is unknown and you don't care about it, look for an assumption that makes it cancel instead of estimating it. The price is that the assumption must hold: real confidence scores are often not calibrated, and if you have raw measurements or a few known edges, it is better to use them.
4

Summing out the network

Sec. II A · Eqs. (6), (7), (18), (24)
\(P(Q, A, g \mid \gamma, \omega)\)sum over A \(P(Q, g \mid \gamma, \omega)\)exact · one pair at a time

Given the groups, every pair is independent. So the sum over all \(2^{\binom{n}{2}}\) networks splits into one two-term sum per pair: the \(A_{ij} = 1\) term plus the \(A_{ij} = 0\) term. What is left is a factor \(F\) per pair that depends on \(Q_{ij}\) and on whether the two nodes share a group. \(F\) is a straight line in \(Q\): at \(Q = 1\) it is the ordinary block-model edge term \(\omega/\rho\), at \(Q = 0\) the non-edge term \((1 - \omega)/(1 - \rho)\), and uncertain reports average the two. It is a sum, not \(\omega^Q (1 - \omega)^{1 - Q}\), because the true state was summed out, not treated as a fraction of an edge.

\[ \begin{aligned} P(Q, g \mid \gamma, \omega) &= \prod_i \gamma_{g_i} \prod_{i<j} F(Q_{ij}, \omega_{g_i g_j}) \\ F(Q, \omega) &= \underbrace{\frac{Q \, \omega}{\rho}}_{A = 1} + \underbrace{\frac{(1 - Q)(1 - \omega)}{1 - \rho}}_{A = 0} \end{aligned} \]

Each observed \(Q\) votes on the groups

\(F = P(Q \mid \omega) / P(Q \mid \rho)\): how much better this pair's block-model edge probability explains \(Q\) than the network average does. Above 1 favours, below 1 disfavours.

…and, given the groups, \(A\) comes back

\(t = P(A_{ij} = 1 \mid Q_{ij}, g_i, g_j)\), Eq. (18): the \(A = 1\) share of \(F\). The diagonal is the raw \(Q\).

Pattern to reuse: sum a latent variable out exactly whenever the model factorizes over it given the others. Here the network is easy once the groups are fixed. The groups are the hard part, because every pair's factor depends on two nodes' groups at once.

The marginal likelihood and EM

Sec. II B · Eqs. (8)–(11), (16), (19) · on a 12-node toy network, where every sum is exact
\(P(Q, g \mid \gamma, \omega)\)sum over g \(P(Q \mid \gamma, \omega)\)maximize \(\hat\gamma, \hat\omega\)exact here · 4,096 terms

The parameters are chosen to maximize the marginal likelihood \(P(Q \mid \gamma, \omega)\), the probability of the data with the groups summed out (Eq. 8). For 12 nodes that sum has \(2^{12} = 4{,}096\) terms, so we can draw it. To keep the picture 1-D, only \(\omega_\text{in}\) is free; \(\omega_\text{out}\) and \(\gamma\) stay at their true values. (With a flat prior on \(\omega\), this maximum-likelihood point is also the MAP estimate.)

EM never climbs \(\log P(Q \mid \omega)\) directly. The E-step computes the posterior \(q(g)\) at the current \(\omega\) and builds the Jensen lower bound (Eq. 11), which touches the curve there. The M-step jumps to the top of the bound. Repeat, and you climb the real curve.

Toy network

Log marginal likelihood and the EM lower bound

Curve: \(\log P(Q \mid \omega_\text{in})\), Eq. (8), up to the \(\beta_0\) constant dropped in Eq. (6). Violet: the bound \(\sum_g q(g) \log\big[P(Q, g \mid \omega_\text{in}) / q(g)\big]\), with \(q\) frozen at the last E-step.

The gap between the curve and the bound is exactly \(\mathrm{KL}\big(q \,\|\, P(g \mid Q, \omega)\big)\). The E-step makes it zero at the current point; the M-step can only raise the curve. Notice what the M-step needs from \(q\): only the probability that each pair of nodes shares a group. The full distribution over 4,096 assignments is never used, which is why belief propagation (Section 7) only has to supply one- and two-node marginals.
6

The posterior over groups

Sec. II B · Eqs. (12)–(15) · same toy network, at the current \(\omega\) from Section 5
\(P(Q, g \mid \hat\gamma, \hat\omega)\)condition on Q (divide by Eq. 8) \(q(g) = P(g \mid Q, \hat\gamma, \hat\omega)\)exact here · BP at scale

The E-step's \(q(g)\) is the answer to "which nodes belong together?". It is a distribution over all \(2^n\) assignments. The model can't tell group A from group B, so every assignment and its mirror image have the same probability. As a result, every single-node marginal \(q_i\) is exactly \(\tfrac12\). The meaningful quantity is the co-membership probability \(P(g_i = g_j \mid Q)\), which doesn't care about labels.

Most probable assignments

Each row is one way to split the 12 nodes. The outlined row is the truth.

Exact co-membership

\(P(g_i = g_j \mid Q)\), summing all 4,096 assignments.

Belief propagation's version

From BP marginals: \(q_i q_j + (1 - q_i)(1 - q_j)\). BP picks one of the two mirror images.

Plugging in \(\omega\) vs. averaging over it

EM hands you \(P(g \mid Q, \hat\omega)\): the posterior at one value of \(\omega\) (empirical Bayes). Full Bayes averages it over \(P(\omega_\text{in} \mid Q)\) instead. On 12 nodes \(\omega\) is poorly pinned down, so this is the hardest case for the plug-in.

\(\omega\) is very uncertain, yet the co-membership barely moves. Per-pair answers are insensitive to \(\omega\) across its plausible range, which is why EM's plug-in is usually fine for them, and with hundreds of nodes \(P(\omega \mid Q)\) is a narrow spike anyway. The plug-in goes wrong in two situations: when \(\hat\omega\) sits far from where the posterior mass is (press Reset in Section 5 and watch the dots leave the diagonal), and for totals over many pairs, where small shifts add up (the edge-count result from the EB-vs-full-Bayes script).

Belief propagation at scale

Sec. II C · Eqs. (27)–(34), (16), (19) · Sec. III A · Fig. 3(a)
\(q(g)\) over \(2^n\) assignmentsapproximate marginals \(q_r^i, q_{rs}^{ij}\)approximate · exact on trees

With 200 nodes the sum in Eq. (14) has 2²⁰⁰ terms. Belief propagation replaces it with messages \(\eta^{i \to j}_r\): node \(i\)'s belief about its own group if \(j\) were removed. It returns exactly the one- and two-node marginals the M-step needs, and EM alternates BP with the parameter updates (Eqs. 16, 19).

Pattern to reuse: when the posterior over a latent is combinatorial, don't compute it. Compute only the marginals your parameter updates need, with message passing, MCMC or a variational family, and know which one you picked: BP is exact on trees and approximate on loopy graphs like this one.
8

Edge recovery

Sec. IV · Eq. (41) · Fig. 5
\(t_{rs}^{ij}\) and \(q_{rs}^{ij}\)sum over groups \(P(A_{ij} = 1 \mid Q)\)uses BP marginals

Finally the network itself. The posterior edge probability averages Eq. (18) over the two nodes' groups: \(P(A_{ij} = 1 \mid Q) = \sum_{rs} t_{rs}^{ij}\, q_{rs}^{ij}\). A pair whose nodes probably share a group is pulled above its raw \(Q\); a pair that probably straddles two groups is pushed below it.

How the model moves each pair

Posterior edge probability against the raw \(Q\), for pairs with \(Q > 0\).

ROC against the true network

Rank pairs by score, declare the top ones edges, sweep the cutoff.

This is a marginal, not a network. Each pair's probability is right on its own, but edges are correlated through the shared groups, so you cannot sample a whole network by flipping these coins independently. Compare Newman 2018, where the edges are independent given \(\theta\) and the marginals are the whole posterior.

Building your own pipeline

The paper's moves, stated generally

Every symbol ends up in one of three places: summed out exactly, approximated, or fixed at a point estimate. Deciding which, symbol by symbol, is the design of the pipeline.

StepIn this paperDistribution you hold afterwardsHow
1. Write the jointSBM × noise model, Eqs. (1), (2)\(P(Q, A, g \mid \gamma, \omega)\)modeling
2. Sort the symbolsobserved \(Q\) · latent \(A, g\) · parameters \(\gamma, \omega\) · nuisance \(\beta_0, \beta_1\)—bookkeeping
3. Cancel nuisancescalibration, Eqs. (3)–(6)\(\beta_0\) becomes a constantexact
4. Sum out easy latents\(A\), one pair at a time, Eq. (7)\(P(Q, g \mid \gamma, \omega)\)exact
5. Posterior of hard latents\(g\), Eq. (14) via BP\(q(g) \approx P(g \mid Q, \gamma, \omega)\), as marginalsapproximate
6. Point-estimate parametersEM on Eq. (8)\(\hat\gamma, \hat\omega\)local max
7. Answer questionsedges, Eq. (41); groups, Eq. (12)\(P(A_{ij} = 1 \mid Q)\), \(q_r^i\)expectations under step 5
Three rules of thumb. (1) If the model factorizes over a variable given the others, sum it out exactly. (2) If summing it out couples everything, approximate its posterior, and only as much of it as later steps use. (3) A few global parameters fitted from many data points can be point-estimated (the empirical-Bayes argument from the EB-vs-full-Bayes script), but totals over the whole network inherit their uncertainty. (4) Write every step with the chain rule in full, then cross variables off one at a time and name the assumption each time: that list is your model.
