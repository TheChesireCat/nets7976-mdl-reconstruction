Bayes on a grid

Carpenter §5 · Laplace's live births, Paris 1745–1770
\(p(\theta)\)multiply by \(p(y \mid \theta)\) \(p(\theta)\, p(y \mid \theta)\)normalize \(p(\theta \mid y)\)exact (conjugate) · and on a grid

Laplace asked whether a birth is more likely to be a boy. Let \(\theta\) be that probability. The data are \(y\) boys out of \(n\) births, so the likelihood is binomial. With a \(\Beta(a, b)\) prior the posterior is again a Beta, which gives an exact answer to check everything else against:

\[ p(\theta \mid y) \;\propto\; \underbrace{\theta^{a-1} (1-\theta)^{b-1}}_{\text{prior}} \cdot \underbrace{\theta^{y} (1-\theta)^{n-y}}_{\text{likelihood}} \;=\; \Beta(a + y,\ b + n - y) \]

With one parameter you can also skip the algebra: evaluate prior × likelihood on a fine grid of \(\theta\) values and divide by the sum. The dots are that grid approximation and the line is the exact Beta.

Prior × likelihood → posterior

The x-axis zooms to where the posterior is. The likelihood is scaled to integrate to 1 so it fits on the same axis.

Summarizing the posterior

Mean, median and mode (nearly the same here), the central 95% interval, and \(\theta = \tfrac12\), the value Laplace's question is about.

Data overwhelm the prior. With 0.01% of the data (about 20 births) the prior sliders move the posterior a lot. Slide to the full data and no reasonable prior matters: \(P(\theta \le \tfrac12)\) drops to about \(10^{-42}\). That is Laplace's answer: boys really are more likely. The grid works because there is only one parameter. With \(d\) parameters a grid needs \(400^d\) points, which is why the rest of the page uses samples.

Monte Carlo: answers from draws

Carpenter §3.3, §5.8–5.9
\(p(\theta \mid y)\)draw \(\theta^{(1)}, \dots, \theta^{(M)}\) \(\mathbb{E}[f(\theta) \mid y] \approx \tfrac{1}{M} \sum_m f(\theta^{(m)})\)error \(\propto 1/\sqrt{M}\)

Every posterior summary is an expectation: the mean is \(\mathbb{E}[\theta]\), and a probability like \(P(\theta \le \tfrac12)\) is \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}(\theta \le \tfrac12)]\). Given independent draws from the posterior, you estimate any of them by averaging. The error shrinks like \(\mathrm{sd} / \sqrt{M}\) whatever the dimension, which a grid cannot do. These draws come from the section 1 posterior.

Histogram of the draws vs. the exact posterior

More draws, closer match. Nothing here uses the formula for the Beta except to draw the line.

The running estimate of the mean

Average of the first \(m\) draws (line), the exact mean (hairline), and the \(\pm 2\) Monte Carlo standard error band.

Counting cannot see rare events. With the full data the estimate of \(P(\theta \le \tfrac12)\) is exactly 0 for any \(M\) you could afford, because the true value is about \(10^{-42}\). Averages of draws are good for things that happen often under the posterior. This page's draws are independent. MCMC draws are not, and the next section is about what that costs.

The toy problem: friends and strangers

The dolphin model with the network taken away · Young et al. §3.1
\(z_i \sim \Bernoulli(\rho)\)then \(x_i \sim \Poisson(\lambda_{z_i})\)observe only \(x\) infer \(\lambda_0, \lambda_1, \rho\) and every \(z_i\)the rest of the page

You have \(N\) counts, each one how often a pair of animals was seen together. A pair is either strangers, who meet by chance about \(\lambda_0\) times, or friends, who meet about \(\lambda_1\) times. A share \(\rho\) of the pairs are friends. The hidden label \(z_i\) says which kind pair \(i\) is:

\[ \begin{aligned} z_i &\sim \Bernoulli(\rho) && \text{is pair } i \text{ friends?} && \text{hidden} \\ x_i \mid z_i &\sim \Poisson(\lambda_{z_i}) && \text{how often they were seen together} && \text{observed} \end{aligned} \]

The inference problem: from the counts alone, find \(\theta = (\lambda_0, \lambda_1, \rho)\) and, for each pair, \(P(z_i = 1 \mid x)\). The top bar sets the true values used to simulate the counts. The samplers never see them, so you can check what they recover.

What you observe

The \(N\) counts from the top bar. Which of them come from friends is exactly what you can't see; §5 colours them in.

\(z\) is discrete, so the samplers never touch it. HMC needs a gradient, so §4 and §5 use the likelihood with z summed out, \(p(x_i \mid \theta) = \rho \Poisson(x_i \mid \lambda_1) + (1 - \rho) \Poisson(x_i \mid \lambda_0)\), and recover the \(z\)'s afterwards. §5 explains why that sum is exact. For the dolphins, pair \(i\) becomes the pair \((i, j)\) and \(z_i\) becomes the edge \(A_{ij}\).
4

When you can't draw directly: Metropolis vs. HMC

Carpenter §1.2, §3.4, §6 · Young et al. §3.1.2, Fig. 3
\(p(\theta \mid x)\) up to a constantMarkov chain correlated draws \(\theta^{(1)}, \theta^{(2)}, \dots\)exact in the limit · check it

To make the posterior visible, this section holds \(\rho\) at its true value (\(z\) is summed out, as in §3), so the posterior of the §3 counts is a 2-D surface over \((\lambda_0, \lambda_1)\) that you can see (background). There is no formula for drawing from it, but a Markov chain can walk it so that its positions, in the long run, are draws from it.

Metropolis proposes a random step and accepts it with probability \(\min\bigl(1,\ p(\text{new}) / p(\text{old})\bigr)\). It is blind to the surface's shape, so its steps must be small, and consecutive draws are highly correlated. HMC, which Stan uses, treats \(-\log p\) as a landscape and flicks a ball across it: a random momentum, then \(L\) small steps of size \(\varepsilon\) that follow the gradient. The ball goes far but stays where the probability is. Four chains start from scattered points, and the first quarter of each run is warmup, which is discarded.

Solid: accepted moves. Faint crosses: rejected proposals. Background: the posterior, stronger colour is more probable.

With only two parameters a well-tuned Metropolis sampler does fine. The gap grows with the number of parameters: on the dolphin model (three to five parameters, with \(\rho\) and \(\lambda_0\) correlated), Metropolis at the same number of iterations still has \(\hat R\) well above 1.01 and an order of magnitude fewer effective draws per unit of work, while HMC has converged.

What "correlated draws" means

Strip a chain down to one number: the correlation \(\phi\) between each draw and the next. Here the target is a standard normal and each draw is \(x_{t+1} = \phi\, x_t + \sqrt{1 - \phi^2}\, \text{noise}\), so every value of \(\phi\) samples the same distribution. Only the order of the draws changes. With \(\phi > 0\) the chain is sticky: each draw repeats much of the last one, as Metropolis with small steps does. With \(\phi < 0\) it zig-zags: a high draw tends to be followed by a low one, as HMC does when a trajectory overshoots the centre and lands on the far side.

The first 60 draws

Same target every time, \(\mathcal{N}(0, 1)\). Only the path through it differs.

Each draw against the next

Tilted up: sticky. Round: independent. Tilted down: zig-zag.

How wrong is the average of 100 draws?

500 independent reruns. Bars: this \(\phi\). Line: what 100 independent draws give.

The effective sample size counts how much the errors cancel. For this chain the autocorrelation time is \(\tau = (1 + \phi)/(1 - \phi)\), and \(\mathrm{ESS} = N/\tau\). Sticky draws (\(\phi = 0.9\)) give \(\tau = 19\): 100 draws are worth about 5 independent ones, and the averages spread widely. Zig-zag draws (\(\phi = -0.7\)) give \(\tau \approx 0.18\): each overshoot is corrected by the next draw, so 100 draws are worth about 570, and the averages are tighter than independent sampling could make them.
Read the tiles, not the traces alone.
• \(\hat R\) compares the chains with each other, and with the two halves of themselves. It is near 1 when they all describe the same distribution, and above about 1.01 means keep going.
• ESS is how many independent draws your correlated ones are worth.
• ESS per 1000 evaluations is the fair comparison, because each HMC step costs L gradient evaluations. HMC's ESS can even exceed the number of draws: its zig-zag autocorrelation means successive draws are negatively correlated, so their errors partly cancel.
Try a huge Metropolis step (almost nothing is accepted), a tiny one (every move is accepted but goes nowhere), or an HMC \(\varepsilon\) so large that the trajectories fly off and get rejected. Then try \(L = 10\): at this \(\varepsilon\) a trajectory curls back to where it started, so the extra work buys nothing. That is why Stan uses NUTS, which picks the length of each trajectory by itself.
5

Summing out a discrete unknown

Carpenter §11 · Young et al. Eqs. (A.12)–(A.14)
\(p(\lambda, \rho, z \mid x)\)sum over z \(p(\lambda, \rho \mid x)\)HMC draws of \((\lambda, \rho)\)condition \(Q_i = P(z_i = 1 \mid x_i, \lambda, \rho)\)exact sum · MCMC

Now \(\rho\) is unknown too, and so is each count's label \(z_i\) (which Poisson it came from). HMC needs a gradient, so it cannot move a discrete \(z\). Stan doesn't allow discrete parameters at all. The fix is to sum each label out. The labels are independent given the parameters, so the sum over all \(2^N\) labellings splits into \(N\) two-term sums:

\[ \begin{aligned} p(x \mid \lambda, \rho) &= \prod_i \bigl[ \rho \Poisson(x_i \mid \lambda_1) + (1 - \rho) \Poisson(x_i \mid \lambda_0) \bigr] \\[8pt] Q_i &= \frac{\rho \Poisson(x_i \mid \lambda_1)}{\rho \Poisson(x_i \mid \lambda_1) + (1 - \rho) \Poisson(x_i \mid \lambda_0)} \end{aligned} \]

After sampling \((\lambda, \rho)\), the labels come back for free: for each draw, \(Q_i\) is the probability that count \(i\) came from the high component. Averaging over draws gives the posterior. \(Q_i\) is exactly Newman's E-step (Week 3) and the paper's Eq. (A.14). The difference is that here it is averaged over the uncertainty in \(\theta\) rather than evaluated at a single \(\hat\theta\).

The counts, and the fitted mixture

Bars: how many counts took each value, split by their true (hidden) label. Line: the model's expected histogram at the posterior mean.

\(Q(x)\): the label, recovered

Posterior probability that a count \(x\) came from the \(\lambda_1\) component (line, 90% band over draws), vs. the true share among these data (dots).

Marginalize, sample, then condition. That is the paper's algorithm. It works here, as it does for the dolphins, because the labels are independent once the parameters are fixed. The uncertain labels are the counts in the overlap between the two Poissons, where \(Q\) is near \(\tfrac12\). Push \(\lambda_0\) and \(\lambda_1\) together to widen the overlap.

Symmetry and label switching

Carpenter §13.2 (identifiability) · Young et al. §3.1.2 ("we have imposed \(\lambda_1 > \lambda_0\)") and §3.2 (\(\beta < \tfrac12 < \alpha\))
\((\lambda_0, \lambda_1, \rho)\)relabel \((\lambda_1, \lambda_0, 1 - \rho)\)same likelihood two identical posterior modesbreak it with a constraint

Swap the names of the two components: call the high one "0" and the low one "1", and replace \(\rho\) with \(1 - \rho\). Every term \(\rho \Poisson(x \mid \lambda_1) + (1 - \rho) \Poisson(x \mid \lambda_0)\) is unchanged, so the data can't tell the two labellings apart. The posterior has two mirror-image modes of exactly the same height, one on each side of the diagonal \(\lambda_0 = \lambda_1\). Nothing is wrong with the data. The labels just mean nothing yet.

Four HMC chains start at random points. Each one climbs into whichever mode is closer and stays there, because the valley between the modes is too deep to cross. Then the pooled draws mix two different "answers":

Where each chain ended up

Post-warmup draws of \((\lambda_0, \lambda_1)\), coloured by chain. The diagonal is \(\lambda_0 = \lambda_1\), the mirror between the modes.

Trace of \(\lambda_1\)

Chains in the mirror mode report the low rate as "\(\lambda_1\)".

Before averaging over \(\theta\), remove the transformations that leave the likelihood unchanged. The pooled "posterior means" land between the modes, describing neither. The pooled \(Q\) for a clearly high count comes out near \(\tfrac12\), which says nothing, when every chain on its own is nearly certain. \(\hat R\) flags it, but only when the chains happen to disagree. If all four land in the same mode (about 1 time in 8) everything looks fine by luck. The constraint \(\lambda_1 > \lambda_0\) is not a hack. It is what gives "component 1" (for the dolphins, "edge") its meaning: connected pairs interact more.

Does the model fit? Posterior-predictive checks

Carpenter §9.5 · Young et al. §3.1.2, Eq. (3.4), Figs. 4–6 · Gelman, Meng & Stern (1996)
draws of \(\theta\)simulate \(\tilde X \sim p(\tilde X \mid \theta)\)compare with the real \(X\) \(D(\tilde X; \theta)\) vs. \(D(X; \theta)\) → \(p\)-valuemodel check

A well-mixed sampler only means you have computed the posterior of your model correctly. It says nothing about whether the model describes the data. To check that, use the fitted model to generate fake data sets, one per posterior draw, and see whether the real data look like one of them. The paper summarizes "look like" with a discrepancy, a KL-like distance between counts and the model's expected counts \(m_i = \sum_k Q_i(k)\, \lambda_k\):

\[ D(X; \theta) = \sum_{x_i > 0} x_i \log \frac{x_i}{m_i(\theta)} \qquad\qquad p = P\bigl( D(\tilde X; \theta) > D(X; \theta) \bigr) \]

The data below come from a truth with a third component (a small group of very high counts) unless you switch it off. Fit it with \(K = 1\), 2 or 3 components and watch the check.

Truth Fit

Real vs. replicated histograms

Bars: the observed counts. Lines: 20 data sets simulated from posterior draws.

Observed vs. predicted, per count

Each dot is one count against its replicate in 5 simulated data sets (the paper's Fig. 4a).

Discrepancy: replicate vs. real

One dot per posterior draw. Above the diagonal the fake data are further from the model than the real data (the paper's Fig. 4b).

Small \(p\) is bad and \(p\) near 1 is suspicious. If the model were the truth, the real data would sit in the middle of the replicates, and \(p\) would be around \(\tfrac12\). With the default truth, \(K = 1\) gives \(p \approx 0\): the real data are worse fit than anything the model would generate. \(K = 2\) lands near the dolphins' 0.136. That doesn't rule the model out, but the replicate histograms and the observed-vs-predicted cloud show where it fails: the highest counts get pulled into one middle component. \(K = 3\) fits. A \(p\) near 1 means the real data fit better than the model's own fake data, which is a warning sign of overfitting. That is what the paper sees for AddHealth (Fig. 6), with two free error rates per student, and why it suggests beta hyperpriors.
