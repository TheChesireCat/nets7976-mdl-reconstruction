Week 3 · Network reconstruction from noisy data

Which distribution are you holding?

Newman measures every pair of nodes \(N\) times and counts how often an edge shows up. Two unknowns sit under those counts: the true network \(A\), and the error rates of the measurement. This page follows one synthetic network through the paper. The structure is simpler than Martin et al.: there are no groups, so given the error rates every pair is independent, and the posterior over networks comes out exactly. The only approximation left is plugging in one estimate of the error rates, and the last section shows what that costs.

Model and data

Eqs. (1), (7), (8)
\(\theta = (\rho, \alpha, \beta)\)sample \(P(A, E \mid \theta)\)forward model

Each pair is an edge with probability \(\rho\). Each of the \(N\) measurements then sees a true edge with probability \(\alpha\) and a non-edge with probability \(\beta\), so the count \(E_{ij}\) is binomial with one of two success rates. Everything the data say about a pair is in its count.

\[ \begin{aligned} P(A, E \mid \theta) &= P(E \mid A, \theta)\, P(A \mid \theta) && \text{(chain rule)} \\ P(E \mid A, \theta) &= \prod_{i<j} \bigl[\alpha^{E_{ij}} (1-\alpha)^{N-E_{ij}}\bigr]^{A_{ij}} \bigl[\beta^{E_{ij}} (1-\beta)^{N-E_{ij}}\bigr]^{1-A_{ij}} && \text{(Eq. 7)} \\ P(A \mid \theta) &= \prod_{i<j} \rho^{A_{ij}} (1-\rho)^{1-A_{ij}} && \text{(only } \rho \text{ enters)} \end{aligned} \]

The middle bars are where inference matters. Pairs seen 0 times or every time are easy. Pairs seen once or twice are a mix of real edges that were often missed and non-edges that were occasionally reported, and only the error rates can say which is more likely.

False positives vs. false discoveries

p. 543 · the false discovery rate \((1 - \rho)\beta / [\rho\alpha + (1 - \rho)\beta]\)
\(P(\text{seen} \mid A = 0) = \beta\)Bayes' rule \(P(A = 0 \mid \text{seen}) = \text{FDR}\)exact

The two rates condition on opposite things. The false-positive rate \(\beta\) asks: of the non-edges, what share get reported? The false discovery rate asks: of the reports, what share are non-edges? Bayes' rule links them through the density \(\rho\), and in a sparse network non-edges vastly outnumber edges, so a tiny \(\beta\) can still produce many false reports.

One measurement, two ways of slicing it

Top: what happens to non-edges. Bottom: where the reported edges come from. Same numbers, different denominator.

The base rate does the damage. Try the Reality-mining values: \(\beta\) is under 0.5%, yet about a quarter of single reports are false, because there are roughly 29 non-edges for every edge. Repeated measurements are what rescue it: a false report is rarely repeated.

Estimating \(\theta\) with EM

Eqs. (1)–(5)
\(P(A, \theta \mid E)\)sum over A \(P(\theta \mid E)\)maximize \(\hat\theta\)exact sum · local max

Newman maximizes \(P(\theta \mid E)\), the posterior of the parameters with the network summed out. With the uniform priors he assumes, that is the same point as the maximum of the likelihood \(P(E \mid \theta)\). The sum over all \(2^{\binom{n}{2}}\) networks is exact here, because the pairs are independent given \(\theta\): it splits into one two-term sum per pair. EM climbs it by alternating the E-step (compute every \(Q_{ij}\) at the current \(\theta\), Eq. 6) and the M-step (re-estimate \(\theta\) from those \(Q\)'s, Eq. 5).

The landscape EM climbs

\(\log P(E \mid \theta)\) over \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), with \(\rho\) fixed at its estimate. Stronger colour is higher. The line is EM's path from its starting guess (projected onto this slice).

Each M-step is "count, weighted by \(Q\)". \(\hat\alpha\) is the hit rate among pairs weighted by how likely each is to be an edge, \(\hat\beta\) the hit rate weighted by how likely each is not to be one, and \(\hat\rho\) the average \(Q\). EM never needs to decide which pairs are edges.

The posterior over networks, for free

Eqs. (3), (6), (12)
\(P(A, \hat\theta \mid E)\)condition (divide by \(P(\hat\theta \mid E)\)) \(P(A \mid E, \hat\theta) = \prod Q_{ij}^{A_{ij}} (1 - Q_{ij})^{1 - A_{ij}}\)exact given \(\hat\theta\)

The E-step's distribution is the posterior over networks at \(\hat\theta\). Because pairs are independent given \(\theta\), it is a product of one coin per pair, and every pair with the same count gets the same coin. So the whole posterior is just \(N + 1\) numbers: \(Q_0, \dots, Q_N\). Each is prior odds times evidence:

\[ \log\frac{Q}{1 - Q} = \underbrace{\log\frac{\rho}{1 - \rho}}_{\text{prior odds}} + \underbrace{E \log\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}_{\text{each report}} + \underbrace{(N - E) \log\frac{1 - \alpha}{1 - \beta}}_{\text{each miss}} \]

Q for every possible count

Posterior edge probability at \(\hat\theta\) (line), and the actual share of true edges among pairs with that count (dots).

Where that Q comes from

The log-odds for the chosen count, built up term by term. Right of zero favours an edge.

Compare Martin et al. There, every pair's factor depended on two nodes' groups, which coupled all the pairs and needed belief propagation. Here nothing couples the pairs once \(\theta\) is fixed, so the exact posterior is a handful of numbers, and you can draw whole networks from it by flipping independent coins.

One network or many?

Supplementary Sec. S5 · and the EB-vs-full-Bayes script
\(P(A \mid E, \hat\theta)\)sample networks, then average \(P(f(A) \mid E)\) for any network measure \(f\)approximate: \(\hat\theta\) plugged in

Two tempting shortcuts, and what they cost. Shortcut 1: keep only the single most probable network (every pair with \(Q > \tfrac{1}{2}\)). Shortcut 2: plug in \(\hat\theta\) and ignore its uncertainty, which is what EM does. The chart compares both with full Bayes, which also averages over \(\theta\) (a Gibbs sampler), on one quantity: the total number of edges.

Do the 90% intervals catch the truth?

Share of freshly generated networks whose true edge count falls inside each method's 90% interval.

Per pair, plugging in \(\hat\theta\) is usually fine; for totals, it is overconfident. At the default settings the two methods' \(Q\)'s agree to within about 0.005, but a small error in \(\theta\) shifts every \(Q\) in the same direction, and across tens of thousands of pairs that adds up. (Per-pair answers diverge too when the data barely pin \(\theta\) down: try \(n = 50\), or \(N = 1\), where only the overall report rate \(\rho\alpha + (1 - \rho)\beta\) is identifiable and \(\rho, \alpha, \beta\) can't be separated.) The single most probable network is worse: it drops every pair with \(Q\) below \(\tfrac{1}{2}\), so with few measurements it can miss a large share of the edges. Lower \(N\) to see it.

Newman vs. Martin

Same recipe, different choices

Both papers write a joint, sum out what factorizes, point-estimate the parameters with EM, and read answers off the posterior. They differ in what the data are and what couples the pairs.

Newman 2018Martin et al. 2016
Datacounts \(E_{ij}\) out of \(N\) measurementsone probability \(Q_{ij}\) per pair
Noise modellearned: \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are parametersremoved: calibration makes \(\beta_0\) cancel

The design choice that decides everything is what couples the unknowns. Independence given the parameters makes the posterior exact and cheap; shared latent structure like communities makes it combinatorial and forces an approximation. Adding groups to Newman's model would turn it into Martin's problem.
