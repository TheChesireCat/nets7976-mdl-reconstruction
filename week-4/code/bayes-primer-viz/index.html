1Bayes on a grid

Carpenter §5

Laplace asked whether a birth is more likely to be a boy.The counts are Laplace's live births in Paris, 1745 to 1770. Let \(\theta\) be that probability. The data are \(y\) boys out of \(n\) births, so the likelihood is binomial. With a Beta prior, \(\Beta(a, b)\), the posterior is again a Beta, which gives an exact answer to check everything else against:

\[ p(\theta \mid y) \;\propto\; \underbrace{\theta^{a-1} (1-\theta)^{b-1}}_{\text{prior}} \cdot \underbrace{\theta^{y} (1-\theta)^{n-y}}_{\text{likelihood}} \;=\; \Beta(a + y,\ b + n - y) \]
\(\displaystyle p(\theta \mid y) \;\propto\; \theta^{a-1} (1-\theta)^{b-1} \cdot \theta^{y} (1-\theta)^{n-y}\)
prior × likelihood
\(\displaystyle \phantom{p(\theta \mid y)} = \Beta(a + y,\ b + n - y)\)
again a Beta
Why does a Beta prior give a Beta posterior?

Write both densities as functions of \(\theta\) and drop every factor that doesn't involve it. The prior contributes \(\theta^{a-1} (1-\theta)^{b-1}\) and the likelihood \(\theta^{y} (1-\theta)^{n-y}\). Multiplying adds the exponents, giving \(\theta^{a+y-1} (1-\theta)^{b+n-y-1}\): the shape of a \(\Beta(a + y,\ b + n - y)\) density. The posterior's normalizing constant is whatever makes it integrate to 1, so it has to be that Beta's. A prior with this property is called conjugate to the likelihood.

With one parameter you can also skip the algebra: evaluate prior × likelihood on a fine grid of \(\theta\) values and divide by the sum. Figure 1 does both, and the two agree.

Data overwhelm the prior. With 0.01% of the data, about 20 births, the prior sliders move the posterior a lot. Slide to the full data and no reasonable prior matters: \(P(\theta \le \tfrac12)\) drops to about \(10^{-42}\). That is Laplace's answer: boys really are more likely.

The grid works because there is only one parameter. With \(d\) parameters a grid needs \(400^d\) points, which is why the rest of the page uses samples.

2Monte Carlo: answers from draws

Carpenter §3.3, §5.8–5.9

Every posterior summary is an expectation: the mean is \(\mathbb{E}[\theta]\), and a probability like \(P(\theta \le \tfrac12)\) is \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}(\theta \le \tfrac12)]\). Given independent draws from the posterior, you estimate any of them by averaging. The error shrinks like \(\mathrm{sd} / \sqrt{M}\) whatever the dimension, which a grid cannot do.This is the Monte Carlo standard error; see Monte Carlo integration. These draws come from the §1 posterior.

Counting cannot see rare events. With the full data the estimate of \(P(\theta \le \tfrac12)\) is exactly 0 for any \(M\) you could afford, because the true value is about \(10^{-42}\). Averages of draws are good for things that happen often under the posterior.

These draws are independent. MCMC draws are not, and §4 is about what that costs.

3The toy problem: friends and strangers

Young et al. §3.1

You have \(N\) counts, each one how often a pair of animals was seen together.This is the dolphin model of Young et al. with the network taken away. A pair is either strangers, who meet by chance about \(\lambda_0\) times, or friends, who meet about \(\lambda_1\) times. A share \(\rho\) of the pairs are friends. The hidden label \(z_i\) says which kind pair \(i\) is, and each count is Poisson with that kind's rate:

\[ \begin{aligned} z_i &\sim \Bernoulli(\rho) && \text{is pair } i \text{ friends?} && \text{hidden} \\ x_i \mid z_i &\sim \Poisson(\lambda_{z_i}) && \text{how often they were seen together} && \text{observed} \end{aligned} \]
\(z_i \sim \Bernoulli(\rho)\)
is pair \(i\) friends? Hidden.
\(x_i \mid z_i \sim \Poisson(\lambda_{z_i})\)
how often they were seen together. Observed.

The inference problem: from the counts alone, find \(\theta = (\lambda_0, \lambda_1, \rho)\) and, for each pair, \(P(z_i = 1 \mid x)\). The strip above Figure 3 sets the true values used to simulate the counts. The samplers never see them, so you can check what they recover.

\(z\) is discrete, so the samplers never touch it. HMC needs a gradient, so §4 and §5 use the likelihood with \(z\) summed out, and recover the \(z\)'s afterwards:

\[ p(x_i \mid \theta) = \rho \Poisson(x_i \mid \lambda_1) + (1 - \rho) \Poisson(x_i \mid \lambda_0) \]

§5 explains why that sum is exact. For the dolphins, pair \(i\) becomes the pair \((i, j)\) and \(z_i\) becomes the edge \(A_{ij}\).

4When you can't draw directly: Metropolis vs. HMC

Carpenter §1.2, §3.4, §6 · Young et al. §3.1.2, Fig. 3

To make the posterior visible, this section holds \(\rho\) at its true value (\(z\) is summed out, as in §3), so the posterior of the §3 counts is a 2-D surface over \((\lambda_0, \lambda_1)\) that you can see. There is no formula for drawing from it, but a Markov chain can walk it so that its positions, in the long run, are draws from it.

Metropolis proposes a random step and accepts it with probability \(\min\bigl(1,\ p(\text{new}) / p(\text{old})\bigr)\). It is blind to the surface's shape, so its steps must be small, and consecutive draws are highly correlated. HMC, which Stan uses, treats \(-\log p\) as a landscape and flicks a ball across it: a random momentum, then \(L\) small steps of size \(\varepsilon\) that follow the gradient. The ball goes far but stays where the probability is. Four chains start from scattered points, and the first quarter of each run is warmup, which is discarded.

At \(L = 10\) and this \(\varepsilon\) a trajectory curls back to where it started, so the extra work buys nothing.That is why Stan uses NUTS, the No-U-Turn Sampler, which picks the length of each trajectory by itself. Two numbers say whether to trust the chains. \(\hat R\) compares the chains with each other, and with the two halves of themselves: it is near 1 when they all describe the same distribution, and above about 1.01 means keep going. The effective sample size (ESS) is how many independent draws the correlated ones are worth.

With only two parameters a well-tuned Metropolis sampler does fine. The gap grows with the number of parameters.On the dolphin model (three to five parameters, with \(\rho\) and \(\lambda_0\) correlated), Metropolis at the same number of iterations still has \(\hat R\) well above 1.01 and an order of magnitude fewer effective draws per unit of work, while HMC has converged.

What does "correlated draws" mean?

Strip a chain down to one number: the correlation \(\phi\) between each draw and the next. Here the target is a standard normal and each draw is \(x_{t+1} = \phi\, x_t + \sqrt{1 - \phi^2}\, \text{noise}\), so every value of \(\phi\) samples the same distribution. Only the order of the draws changes. With \(\phi > 0\) the chain is sticky: each draw repeats much of the last one, as Metropolis with small steps does. With \(\phi < 0\) it zig-zags: a high draw tends to be followed by a low one, as HMC does when a trajectory overshoots the centre and lands on the far side.

The effective sample size counts how much the errors cancel. For this chain the autocorrelation time is \(\tau = (1 + \phi)/(1 - \phi)\), and \(\mathrm{ESS} = N/\tau\). Sticky draws (\(\phi = 0.9\)) give \(\tau = 19\): 100 draws are worth about 5 independent ones, and the averages spread widely. Zig-zag draws (\(\phi = -0.7\)) give \(\tau \approx 0.18\): each overshoot is corrected by the next draw, so 100 draws are worth about 570, and the averages are tighter than independent sampling could make them.

That is why HMC's ESS can exceed the number of draws: its zig-zag autocorrelation means successive draws are negatively correlated, so their errors partly cancel.

5Summing out a discrete unknown

Carpenter §11 · Young et al. Eqs. (A.12)–(A.14)

Now \(\rho\) is unknown too, and so is each count's label \(z_i\) (which Poisson it came from). HMC needs a gradient, so it cannot move a discrete \(z\), and Stan doesn't allow discrete parameters at all. The fix is to sum each label out, which leaves the likelihood of a two-component mixture. The labels are independent given the parameters, so the sum over all \(2^N\) labellings splits into \(N\) two-term sums:

\[ \begin{aligned} p(x \mid \lambda, \rho) &= \prod_i \bigl[ \rho \Poisson(x_i \mid \lambda_1) + (1 - \rho) \Poisson(x_i \mid \lambda_0) \bigr] \\[8pt] Q_i &= \frac{\rho \Poisson(x_i \mid \lambda_1)}{\rho \Poisson(x_i \mid \lambda_1) + (1 - \rho) \Poisson(x_i \mid \lambda_0)} \end{aligned} \]

After sampling \((\lambda, \rho)\), the labels come back for free: for each draw, \(Q_i\) is the probability that count \(i\) came from the high component. Averaging over draws gives the posterior. \(Q_i\) is the paper's Eq. (A.14).It is also exactly the E-step of EM in Newman's Week 3 article, which evaluates it at a single \(\hat\theta\) instead of averaging over \(\theta\).

Marginalize, sample, then condition. That is the paper's algorithm. It works here, as it does for the dolphins, because the labels are independent once the parameters are fixed. The uncertain labels are the counts in the overlap between the two Poissons, where \(Q\) is near \(\tfrac12\).

6Symmetry and label switching

Carpenter §13.2 · Young et al. §3.1.2, §3.2

Swap the names of the two components: call the high one "0" and the low one "1", and replace \(\rho\) with \(1 - \rho\). Every term \(\rho \Poisson(x \mid \lambda_1) + (1 - \rho) \Poisson(x \mid \lambda_0)\) is unchanged, so the data can't tell the two labellings apart: the labels are not identifiable. The posterior has two mirror-image modes of exactly the same height, one on each side of the diagonal \(\lambda_0 = \lambda_1\). Nothing is wrong with the data. The labels just mean nothing yet, and samplers that wander between the mirror images are said to suffer from label switching.

Four HMC chains start at random points. Each one climbs into whichever mode is closer and stays there, because the valley between the modes is too deep to cross. Then the pooled draws mix two different "answers".

Before averaging over \(\theta\), remove the transformations that leave the likelihood unchanged. The pooled "posterior means" land between the modes, describing neither. The pooled \(Q\) for a clearly high count comes out near \(\tfrac12\), which says nothing, when every chain on its own is nearly certain. \(\hat R\) flags it, but only when the chains happen to disagree: if all four land in the same mode (about 1 time in 8) everything looks fine by luck.

The constraint \(\lambda_1 > \lambda_0\) is not a hack.In Stan it is the positive_ordered type. Young et al. impose \(\lambda_1 > \lambda_0\) (§3.1.2), and \(\beta < \tfrac12 < \alpha\) for AddHealth (§3.2). It is what gives "component 1" (for the dolphins, "edge") its meaning: connected pairs interact more.

7Does the model fit? Posterior-predictive checks

Carpenter §9.5 · Young et al. §3.1.2, Eq. (3.4), Figs. 4–6

A well-mixed sampler only means you have computed the posterior of your model correctly. It says nothing about whether the model describes the data. To check that, run a posterior-predictive check: use the fitted model to generate fake data sets, one per posterior draw, and see whether the real data look like one of them. The paper summarizes "look like" with a discrepancy, a KL-like distance between counts and the model's expected counts \(m_i = \sum_k Q_i(k)\, \lambda_k\), and a \(p\)-value.The realized-discrepancy \(p\)-value is due to .

\[ D(X; \theta) = \sum_{x_i > 0} x_i \log \frac{x_i}{m_i(\theta)} \qquad\qquad p = P\bigl( D(\tilde X; \theta) > D(X; \theta) \bigr) \]

The data below come from a truth with a third component (a small group of very high counts) unless you switch it off. Fit it with \(K = 1\), 2 or 3 components and watch the check.

Small \(p\) is bad and \(p\) near 1 is suspicious. If the model were the truth, the real data would sit in the middle of the replicates, and \(p\) would be around \(\tfrac12\). With the default truth, \(K = 1\) gives \(p \approx 0\): the real data are worse fit than anything the model would generate. \(K = 2\) lands near the dolphins' 0.136. That doesn't rule the model out, but the replicate histograms and the observed-vs-predicted cloud show where it fails: the highest counts get pulled into one middle component. \(K = 3\) fits.

A \(p\) near 1 means the real data fit better than the model's own fake data, which is a warning sign of overfitting.That is what the paper sees for AddHealth (Fig. 6), with two free error rates per student, and why it suggests beta hyperpriors.

The only thing the network adds is meaning. The mathematics is the same mixture. What changes is that a sample of the \(z\)'s is now a sample of a network, so you can compute any network property and get its full posterior. The dolphin article does exactly that with the real counts.
